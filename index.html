Honeybee Bakery: a website redesign case study

I redesigned the website for Honeybee Bakery, a fictional neighborhood bakery, to show how I audit a dated site, make design decisions for real visitors, and build the result by hand in HTML and CSS.

Honest framing: This is a concept redesign of a fictional business, created for my portfolio. There was no client, no live site, and no measured results — so I have not invented metrics or customer outcomes. What is real is the process, the reasoning, and the build.

View the redesigned site View the original site

Overview

The original Honeybee site had good bones — a warm business, a clear menu, and loyal regulars — buried under a layout that made all of it hard to find. Everything competed for attention at once, so nothing stood out.

My goal was not to make it look newer for its own sake. It was to answer the three questions most bakery visitors actually arrive with — what do you make, when are you open, and where are you? — in the first screen, on a phone, without hunting.

The problem: what the old site got wrong

Everything shouted, so nothing landed

Star banners, multiple badges, exclamation-heavy copy, and competing colored boxes all fought for the first glance. A new visitor could not tell what mattered most.

The basics were buried

Hours, address, and ordering information sat in a dense table at the very bottom. On a phone — how most people check a bakery — that meant scrolling past everything else first.

Cramped, tiny, and hard to scan

Small type, tight spacing, dashed borders, and a busy striped background made even short pages feel long. Menu items ran together instead of reading as individual choices.

No clear next step

The loudest link was “click here for directions,” while menu and custom-cake ordering — the actions that make the bakery money — had no clear path at all.

Important details hid in fine print

Allergy information and ordering notice were tucked into tiny disclaimer text, exactly where someone who needs them is least likely to look.

It did not feel like the bakery

Clashing primary colors said “generic template,” not warm bread and honey. The site undersold the one thing the business has that a chain does not: it feels personal.

Goals

For visitors

  • Find hours, location, and menu highlights within seconds
  • Read comfortably on a phone, one-handed, in bright light
  • See allergy and ordering information before they need to ask

For the business

  • Lead with the signature products that drive repeat visits
  • Make custom-cake enquiries an obvious next step
  • Look like the warm, trusted neighborhood place it already is

Process

1 · Audit

Read the old site like a visitor

I went through the original page task by task — find the hours, find a price, figure out how to order a cake — and noted every point where I had to search, squint, or guess. That list became the problem section above.

2 · Structure

Sitemap and wireframe thinking

I kept one page but reordered it by visitor priority: hero with the promise, menu highlights, about, testimonials, then a visit block with hours and location. Wireframe first, decoration last — if the order is wrong, no palette fixes it.

3 · Visual direction

Warm, plain, and appetizing

Honey gold, cream, and warm brown, drawn from the products themselves. One serif for body warmth and one clean sans for headings and labels, generous spacing, and rounded cards instead of hard ruled boxes.

4 · Build

Hand-built, mobile-first

I built the redesign in semantic HTML and one shared CSS file, starting from the smallest screen and layering up. CSS color blocks stand in for photography, so the demo stays self-contained with no external images.

Key changes — and why

Hero answers the main question first

Change: One clear headline and two actions — menu and visit — replace the banner clutter. Why: a visitor deciding in seconds needs the promise and the next step, not five competing announcements.

Visit information became a destination, not a footnote

Change: Hours and address moved into a high-contrast “Visit us” panel linked from the top navigation. Why: hours are the most time-sensitive fact on a local business site; burying them costs real visits.

Menu items became individual cards

Change: Each highlight gets its own card, a plain-language description, and a visible price. Why: people choose between items — the layout should mirror that decision instead of merging everything into one block.

Type and spacing grew up

Change: Larger body text, longer line height, and real white space between sections. Why: readability is the feature. If regulars include parents holding a toddler and a phone, small cramped type is a barrier, not a style.

Allergy and ordering notes surfaced

Change: Both moved from tiny print into the menu section at normal size. Why: information people act on for safety or planning belongs where they are already looking.

One warm palette, used consistently

Change: Clashing red/blue/green gave way to honey, cream, and brown throughout. Why: consistency is how a small business looks trustworthy — and the colors now come from the food, not a template.

What I learned

The hardest part was not the new design — it was resisting the urge to add things. The old site's problem was abundance, not absence, so most of my best decisions were removals and reordering. I also learned how much hierarchy work a palette quietly does: once the colors calmed down, the content needed far less formatting to feel organized.

What I would test next

  • Whether a “Order a custom cake” action in the hero earns more enquiries than the menu-first layout
  • Real photography in place of the CSS color blocks, and whether it slows the page on mid-range phones
  • Hours in the header on mobile, tested against the current visit panel
  • A short online order form, scoped carefully so it does not overwhelm a small team
